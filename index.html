Why a dam engineer lives with piezometers

The instrument. A standpipe piezometer is a tube with a porous tip. Water rises in it to the total head at the tip, and the pressure head is simply the height of water above the tip. The tubes drawn here are exactly that. The Svartevann dam on the slides has dozens of them in its core, read for decades.

The rule. Piezometer readings should follow the reservoir. A reading that rises while the reservoir does not, or a tube that suddenly runs clear then muddy, means water is finding a new path: internal erosion, the failure mode that destroyed the Teton Dam in Idaho in 1976 on its first filling, eleven people died. Australian dam owners read their piezometers weekly to monthly under ANCOLD guidelines and compare each one against its expected range.

The design. Everything in the panel on the right is a real design choice: a clay core to cut the seepage, a chimney drain with a filter to catch the water before it reaches the downstream face, a cut-off in the foundation, flatter slopes. The flow net is the drawing engineers used for this before computers, and the number of flow channels in it is still the quickest check of a computer result.
