Darcy's law through layers: across or along?

The permeameter from Example 2, with a cylinder that can hold two soils: one after the other, so the water has to cross both, or side by side, so it runs along both. Why the ground is easier to cross one way than the other

Across the layers (series). The same water must pass through every layer, so the discharge velocity v is the same in each and k1i1 = k2i2: the gradients are in inverse proportion to the permeabilities. Each layer's share of the head is its share of the resistance Lj/kj, and keq = L / Σ(Lj/kj) is pulled towards the tight value: one thin clay seam cuts the vertical permeability of a sand by a thousand.

Along the layers (parallel). Every layer sees the same head drop, so the gradients are equal and the flows add in proportion to kjAj: keq = Σ(kjAj)/A, dominated by the most permeable layer. A thin gravel seam carries the water for the whole deposit.

On site. Natural deposits are laid down in layers, so horizontal permeability is typically 2 to 10 times the vertical, and hundreds of times where clay seams are present. That is why a dam on a layered foundation leaks sideways under the dam rather than down, why an excavation is fed along the sand seams, and why a consolidating clay drains far faster if the borehole missed a silt parting. The two formulas on this page are the first thing an engineer applies to a borehole log.
